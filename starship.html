How do you replace a rocket that never fails?

drag to spin · starship, to scale

falcon 9: 650+ flights · >99% success · and spacex is retiring it anyway

scroll

SpaceX is mid-metamorphosis.

Since its June 2026 IPO the group runs three businesses at once: Connectivity (Starlink), Space (launch), and AI (compute, after absorbing xAI). $18.7B of revenue — and a $2.6B net loss, because everything is being rebuilt at the same time.

Two changes are underway. Neither asks whether. Both ask how fast.

Change 1 · the rocket

Retire Falcon 9. Bet everything on Starship.

Falcon 9 passed 650 flights at >99% success — then SpaceX closed its order book beyond 2028. Starship carries 25–30× the Starlink capacity per flight, and the new V3 satellites are too big for anything else. But its Block 3 booster has never been caught.

Change 2 · the orbit bet

Put the data centres in space.

SpaceX has asked regulators for up to one million AI-compute satellites and unveiled the first, AI1, in June 2026. Deployment "as early as 2028." Alphabet and Anthropic are already paying $2.2B a month for the ground-based compute that precedes it.

research question 1 · accelerate the switch?

The evidence splits exactly down the middle.

Porter adds the part the arithmetic misses: while both rockets fly, SpaceX is stuck in the middle — paying for two competitive positions and holding neither. The Space division lost more than its entire revenue in Q1 2026. The longer the transition, the longer the exposure.

Verdict · RQ1

Hold the July 2026 position — no new Falcon 9 bookings beyond 2028 — but don't retire it early, and keep the cost of turning back under ~25%. Commit publicly; keep the road home cheap.

research question 2 · data centres in orbit by 2028?

The riskiest square on Ansoff's board — and a payback claim that doesn't survive arithmetic.

The project is related diversification — it borrows Starlink's satellite body, lasers and cooling, and SpaceX launches it itself. That makes it cheaper to attempt, and more dependent on Starship. It doesn't spread risk. It concentrates it.

Verdict · RQ2

Keep building — don't commit to 2028. Release capital in stages, gated on Starship milestones, not calendar dates. The revenue can be cancelled in 90 days; the satellites can't be un-launched.

Six tools, chosen separately, kept finding the same number.

That convergence is the paper's real finding. When methods chosen for different purposes keep isolating the same variable, that variable is the decision. The orbit bet cannot move faster than the rocket beneath it — the lower change sets the timetable for the one above.

the why

Let what's proven set the pace — not what's announced.

Commit publicly, so the organisation unfreezes. Keep reversal cheap, because a 2% margin is not conviction. Fund each stage only when the stage below it has worked. That's how you steer two changes stacked on one rocket — and it's a rule that outlives this company and this decade.
